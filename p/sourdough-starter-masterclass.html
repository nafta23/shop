You do not need a sourdough-specific kitchen to start. What you need is cleanliness, temperature control, and a place to breathe. The Vessel: A glass jar is non-negotiable. Look for one with a capacity of 500ml to 1L. Glass allows you to watch the fermentation happen—the bubbles, the rise, the slight dome. Avoid metal containers; the acidity of sourdough can react with them. A loose-fitting lid or a coffee filter secured with a rubber band is perfect. Your starter needs to "breathe," but it also needs to be protected from fruit flies and dust. The Scale: This is the one piece of equipment that separates a hobbyist from a frustrated beginner. Eyeballing flour and water works for pancakes, but sourdough is chemistry. A digital kitchen scale that measures to the gram is essential. You will be measuring a 1:1:1 or 1:2:2 ratio (starter:flour:water by weight). If you don't have a scale, you can use volume measurements (e.g., ½ cup starter, 1 cup flour, ½ cup water), but expect more variability in your results. The Flour: Start with something predictable. All-purpose or bread flour is best for a first activation. Whole wheat or rye flour contains more wild yeast and bacteria, which can speed up the process, but it also creates a messier, stickier starter that is harder to judge for a beginner. Stick to white flour for Day 1. The Water: Tap water is usually fine, but if you have heavily chlorinated water, let it sit uncovered for 24 hours before using, or use filtered water.
