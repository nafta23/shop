Autolyse is the intentional pause after mixing flour and water but before adding salt and leavening. During this window, enzymes begin breaking down starches into simple sugars, and gluten proteins start to align and bond. The result is a dough that feels smoother, stretches further, and requires less aggressive kneading later. For sourdough bakers, it is the first quiet moment in a process that often feels chaotic. The biological shift during autolyse is subtle but cumulative. Amylase enzymes convert complex starches into maltose, which feeds the wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria once the starter is introduced. Simultaneously, glutenin and gliadin proteins hydrate and form a network that will later trap gas. Because this happens before salt is added—which naturally tightens the gluten structure—the dough can develop strength more efficiently. The net effect: a more open crumb, better volume, and a dough that feels alive rather than sluggish. A baker preparing a standard 500 g flour loaf might use 500 g flour + 380 g water for the autolyse, reserving 20 g water for the final mix. After 20 minutes of rest, the dough will appear slightly more cohesive and less shaggy. When the levain and salt are added, the dough comes together with fewer folds needed to reach the “windowpane” stage. The interval between autolyse and bulk fermentation is often treated as a passive waiting period. In this framework, it is treated as an active transition ritual.
